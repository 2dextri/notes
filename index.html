7.01 NOTES - Huang River

What Impact Did Geography Have on Ancient China?


In ancient Egypt, Mesopotamia, and the Indus Valley, early farmers were drawn to rivers. When rivers flooded, fertile silt was deposited on the land. In such rich soil, farmers could grow many crops. The same was true in ancient China.

China has two major rivers. One is the Huang, in the north. The other is the Chang, in the south. Both start in the mountains of western China. From there they flow eastward to the sea. Ancient Chinese civilization arose along those rivers and their tributaries. It arose in more than one place at around the same time, but most known settlements lay along the Huang River.

Huang means "yellow." The river got that name because it looks yellow. The color comes from the vast amounts of fine yellow silt carried by its waters. The Huang picks up that silt as it flows through the Gobi Desert and the Loess Plateau. The silt itself is known as loess. It is very rich-perfect for farming.

In the east, China’s rivers flow across fairly flat plains. Much of the rest of China is covered by highlands and deserts. In ancient times these landforms created barriers to travel. One mountain range, the Himalayas, formed a wall between China and India. The Taklamakan Desert limited passage to the west. So did the mountains surrounding it. The Gobi Desert restricted travel to the north.

How Did the Shang Dynasty Arise in Ancient China?

Farming led to civilization growth in ancient China, as it had in earlier civilizations. Families settled near rivers. Over time, groups of families chose to live and farm in the same place. They formed villages. The rich soil helped them produce lots of food. Populations soared, and villages grew into cities. Strong leaders arose to help organize, govern, and defend the cities.

Around 1600 BCE, one such leader gained control of a number of nearby groups. He was the first of the Shang kings. Like the Egyptians of the Nile River Valley, ancient China was ruled by a series of dynasties. The Shang was the first Chinese dynasty to appear in written records.

The Shang kings were warriors. They expanded their lands through conquest. They were well-organized and well-armed. Their bronze helmets, knives, axes, and bronze-tipped spears gave them an advantage over other nearby peoples. So did their horse-drawn chariots.

Warfare brought the king and his nobles great wealth. Some of it came in the form of plunder. They also gained riches from tribute. Wealth brought power, which helped the dynasty survive. Like the Egyptians, the Shang kingship passed from father to son or brother to brother. This method of holding on to power worked well. The Shang dynasty lasted for nearly 600 years. In 1046 BCE, the last Shang king was finally overthrown. Another dynasty, the Zhou, took over.

>What Was Shang Society Like?

A civilization is a complex society. In China during the Shang era, the king held the top rank. Unlike other ancient river civilizations, the Chinese did not view their kings as gods. The king was the most important and powerful person. A class of wealthy nobles backed the king. Priests, too, were part of this upper class. The king, his family, and the other elite members of society all lived in a walled area at the center of the capital city.

The common people lived at a distance, both physically and socially. Their small villages sat beyond the city walls and throughout the rest of the Huang River valley. They formed a lower class consisting of farmers and artisans. Their main task was to support the elite. In their fields and shops they produced foods and luxury goods for the upper classes. Slaves did not form a large part of Shang society. They were mainly war captives.

Kings used warfare to gain wealth and expand their power. But kings were not just warriors. They were also high priests. Shang religion was polytheistic. Kings offered sacrifices to the many gods. These included gods of the sun and moon as well as the winds, streams, and mountains. The ancient Chinese did not think of their kings as gods. But when kings died, they got special treatment. They were buried in huge tombs with weapons, polished jade, and bronze vessels. Other humans, too, were buried with the king. Most were slaves who had been sacrificed.

The Shang believed that the dead king’s spirit went to a kind of heaven. There it joined the spirits of other ancestors. Ancestors, they thought, had the power make their lives better or worse. The Shang wanted to keep their ancestors happy. So they worshiped their ancestors as well as their gods. Ancestor worship involved making ritual sacrifices. These offerings included wine and cooked foods as well as animals and human beings. The king, as high priest, often performed the rituals.

Shang kings also "talked" with ancestors. They did this through oracle bones. The king might have asked, "Will we win the battle?" or "Is this a good time to plant?". A priest chose a flat animal bone or a tortoise shell. He heated this oracle bone until it cracked. The king interpreted the cracks to "read" the ancestor’s response. A scribe often wrote the answer on the oracle bone with a sharp instrument.

What Were Some Key Contributions of the Shang Dynasty?

Oracle bones show that the Shang had their own form of writing. Like writing in Mesopotamia, it was based on pictures. Each picture stood for a word. We call these pictures characters. Shang writing had about 2,500 characters. Today, the Chinese language has more than 70,000. The Shang also had a calendar. To create it, they studied the moon and the sun. Their calendar had 12 months and 365.25 days per year. It was more precise than the ones used by the other great civilizations. The Shang figured out that a month was 29.5305106 days long. Scientists of today figure a similar number.

Long before the Shang dynasty, Chinese farmers raised silkworms. They turned the silkworms’ cocoons into fine silk cloth. In Shang cities, silk making was an industry. Weavers made silk in workshops just outside the city walls.

The Shang dynasty arose during the Bronze Age. Its skilled crafters learned how to mix copper and tin to make bronze. With this metal, using a process called bronze casting, they formed bowls and pots. Priests used most of these bronze containers in religious rituals. The designs on these objects were complex and beautiful. Bronze crafters also made bells, bronze rings, weapons, and tools.

What Activities Formed the Basis of the Shang Dynasty’s Economy?

Most farmers in Shang times could not afford bronze tools. They used tools made of stone. But they were still able to produce plenty of food. The loess soil was rich and easy to cultivate. It encouraged farming.

As in all river valley civilizations, agriculture played a key role in the economy. Farmers provided food for themselves and for members of the upper class. In the cool north, near the Huang River, most farmers grew millet, a type of grain. In the warmer south, near the Chang River, they grew mainly rice.

Shang armies stole many fine objects in raids on other peoples. But the Huang River civilization did not rely only on warfare to obtain goods. Unlike the Egyptians, they engaged in limited trade, mainly with nearby towns. Shang traders had surplus grain to offer in trade. They also had bronze objects, pottery, and silk. Through barter, they acquired tortoise shells from the Chang River and cowry shells from the southern seacoast.

The Shang also traded to obtain the copper and tin for making bronze. These came from far-off mining regions. So did other raw materials. One was jade. Expert carvers turned this yellowish or greenish stone into beautiful objects that Shang nobles valued highly. Those objects were often buried with their owners.

7.02 NOTES - Chinese Dynasties

How Did the Zhou Dynasty Come into Power?

The Shang Dynasty started out with a good and fair leader. Over 550 years later, the rulers were not good to the people. The last one, Di Xin, tortured and oppressed everyone who was noble. He played favorites among the nobility. This caused them to fight with each other.King Wu of the Zhou saw the problems under Di Xin. He decided to seize control. He met with almost no resistance. In fact, many of Di Xin’s soldiers refused to fight! In order to build more support, Wu and his people opened the prisons, freeing the prisoners. He also gave food from the city’s granaries to the local people. He even took the riches from the palace and gave them out to the soldiers and the people as rewards.

What Is the Mandate of Heaven?

The Zhou argued that they had been given control of the country because of a Mandate of Heaven. This stated that their overthrow of the Shang was the will of the gods. And as long as it was the will of the gods, they would continue to rule.

From kings to emperors, many rulers have claimed that their rule was "God's will." European kings claimed a "Divine Right to rule." Chinese Emperors said they had a "Mandate from Heaven." While these two concepts are similar, there are some differences. View this interactive to learn more about the similarities and differences.

What Is the Dynastic Cycle?

The Zhou argued that they ruled by a Mandate of Heaven. That mandate held as long as the kings and emperors ruled well. When they stopped ruling well, that mandate could be taken away and given to someone else

The Dynastic Cycle shows how this Mandate of Heaven could explain a change in leadership.

(A Dynastic Cycle is what causes one dynasty to take control, thrive, and eventually fall to another dynasty.)

How Long Did the Dynastic Cycle Continue?

After the fall of the Zhou, a new dynasty rose to power. The Qin began work on one of China’s most famous features—the Great Wall of China. The dynastic cycle continued for over a thousand years. After the Qin came the Han, Tang, Song, and Ming dynasties. Each dynasty made advancements in ideas, technology, and more. Eventually, China became a unified empire.

7.03 NOTES - Beliefs of Ancient China

What Did People in Ancient China Believe?


Before the Warring States Period, the people of ancient China had created a belief system. This system shaped the society. It shaped the way in which the people thought about family. It also affected how the society was governed. Explore the slideshow to learn more about Chinese society and beliefs.

When Was China’s Classical Period?

During and after the Warring States Period, Chinese beliefs began to evolve. To evolve means to go through a process of change. However, these changes are not usually quick changes. Changes to China’s belief system were surely not quick. They took place over a long period of time. This time is often called the Classical period. Classical China lasted from 660 BCE to 220 CE. This period is known for its growth and creativity. During this period, China had several great philosophers. Their writings helped to shape China’s culture. Three dynasties or kingdoms made up this period. The three dynasties were the Zhou (pronounced "Joe"), the Qin (pronounced "Chin"), and the Han. During these three dynasties, the country grew in size. Big changes also happened in the Chinese government and economy.

The Zhou dynasty (1029–258 BCE) united the country by making agreements with neighboring areas. As a result, the country grew in land area. There was no strong central government at this time. Each region had its own local ruler. This form of government lasted several hundred years. As time passed, the local rulers became powerful. In time, they overthrew the central Zhou government. The time between the fall of the Zhou and the rise of the next dynasty is when the Warring States Period occurred. The Qin dynasty (221–202 BCE) was the next dynasty to take power. These leaders continued growing the country’s size by taking over nearby lands. The Han dynasty (202 BCE–220 CE) was the last dynasty of the Classical period. It was during the Han dynasty that China fell as a great country. Invaders would take over the country. Each dynasty brought about great change. Leaders looked for ways to respond to that change. They needed new ways to lead the people and build order. Many leaders looked to great thinkers for new ideas.

How Did Society’s Structure Change During the Warring States Period?

During the Warring States Period, several different kingdoms and rulers took control. For example, a set of leaders known as the Chu took over in the 600s BCE. The Chu made changes to the class structure in China. During the Zhou dynasty, the family was at the center of everything. There were three classes of families. The king’s family was at the top of the pyramid. The noble families were in the next class. Finally, the peasant families were in the bottom class. Families from each class were expected to show kindness to families from other classes. This system is called feudalism. The pyramid describes the social structure under the Zhou leaders.

When the Chu took power, they came up with new ways of doing things. The Chu decided that kings should no longer give land to the nobles. Instead they set up a system of bureaucracy. This meant that the kings appointed people to leadership roles in the government. Land was divided into special areas called counties. The Chu kings chose people to lead those counties based on their skills. Other leaders during this time decided that the system of virtues was not working. These leaders thought that society needed a set of written laws to keep order. These new ideas angered some Chinese people. Important thinkers such as Confucius disagreed with the Chu. Confucius believed that making families the center of society would help bring back order.

What Did Confucius Say?

Confucius is one of China’s most famous philosophers. Confucius saw many things he did not like about society. He began to think about ways to improve society. As a boy, Confucius was not allowed to go to school because he was poor. But he was determined to learn all that he could. He went to work for a nobleman who traveled. The nobleman took Confucius with him on his travels. Confucius became known as the smartest man of his time. He began to privately teach anyone who wanted to learn.

Confucius helped restructure the government. Many of Confucius’s thoughts about the government had to do with his beliefs about family. As you have learned, in ancient China, family and ancestors were highly respected. In China, the oldest male was the head of the family. He was to be respected by the entire family. Children were taught and expected to obey their parents. Children did not want to do anything that would embarrass their father and mother.

Ancestor worship was also important. If a child’s parents were still alive, they were expected to take care of them. If the child’s parents died, they were expected to say prayers to them. They were also expected to put offerings on an altar for them. Forgetting your ancestors was seen as disrespectful. Respect for one’s parents and ancestors is known as filial piety. Confucius believed that practicing filial piety was key to having a great society. If people treated their leaders with the same respect they treat their ancestors, then they would become loyal citizens. Confucius felt that leaders should follow these same teachings. If leaders treated citizens with the same love and respect they might treat their children, then their citizens would love them. Long before the Shang dynasty, Chinese farmers raised silkworms. They turned the silkworms’ cocoons into fine silk cloth. In Shang cities, silk making was an industry. Weavers made silk in workshops just outside the city walls.

The Shang dynasty arose during the Bronze Age. Its skilled crafters learned how to mix copper and tin to make bronze. With this metal, using a process called bronze casting, they formed bowls and pots. Priests used most of these bronze containers in religious rituals. The designs on these objects were complex and beautiful. Bronze crafters also made bells, bronze rings, weapons, and tools.

Confucius taught that people are part of five important relationships. These include:

- king to subject

- father to son

- husband to wife

- older brother to younger brother

- friend to friend

Confucius pushed people to build good relationships in these five areas. By doing so, society would become strong. Confucius wrote down his ideas and thoughts. His followers later put all his ideas into one book. The Analects ( The Analects is a book of literature that contains the writings of Confucius ) is a group of Confucius's writings. They explain how Confucius thought people should live and treat one another.

Confucianism is not a religion but a way of living. Respect for oneself and for others is at the heart of Confucianism.

Who Was Laozi?

Laozi (Creator of Daoism) was another teacher who lived at the same time as Confucius. Like Confucius, Laozi also wrote down his ideas. His book of teachings is known as the Dao De Jing. Daoism, or Taoism, is the name of the religion begun by Laozi. Its name means "way" or "path." Unlike Confucius, Laozi taught that a person needed to do more than just be kind to his fellow man. He believed that both living and non-living things had a force. It is through connecting with this force that one finds happiness. Laozi taught the idea of wu-wei, or letting nature take its course.

Because Laozi taught that people should let nature take its course, he did not believe in strict governments. Governments that wrote down strict laws were trying to make everyone behave the same way. This, according to Laozi, goes against human nature. People should be free to make their own decisions.

Laozi believed that every living thing had a life-force. The life-force had two sides, which are known as the yin and the yang. The image on the screen shows the classic yin-yang symbol. The yin, or the dark side, included women, the moon, and death. The yang, or light side, included men, the sun, rivers, and birth. Laozi thought that every person was made up of some yin and some yang. The life-force should be protected at all costs. Therefore, people should try to avoid war and killing others.

Some people practice both Confucianism and Daoism. Confucianism teaches how to act toward others while Daoism teaches how to act toward oneself and nature. What Was Legalism?

After the Zhou fell, the Qin dynasty united China once again. These people created the first Chinese empire. The Qin dynasty had the first emperor or king of China. This dynasty only lasted 15 years, but many things happened in this short period of time.

The first emperor of the Qin was known as Qin Shi Huangdi. His advisors encouraged him to follow the teachings of Confucius. However, the emperor did not take this advice. He and a group of new advisors created a new system of leading the empire called Legalism. Legalism is a system in which laws are strictly followed. It was developed by Han Fei Tzu. Han Fei Tzu (280-233 BCE) was a legalist and philosopher who lived during the Han dynasty. Legalism also uses bureaucracy. The emperor believed that he should have strict control of the government. Everyone, rich and poor, followed his code of law. He also set up a police force to make sure the laws were followed.

The emperor had great power because the people feared being punished. The emperor took land away from the nobles. He thought if the nobles got too much power they would try to overtake his government. He did not give the land to the poor but kept it for himself. The emperor also thought that books were useless. He ordered that all books be burned. This is known as censorship. The only books that could be saved were books on medicine and farming.

Han Fei Tzu’s ideas differed from Confucius's ideas. Remember that Confucius believed that government should be based on respect. Han Fei Tzu argued that rulers should rely on laws and rules.
